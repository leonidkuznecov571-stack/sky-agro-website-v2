Malt & Barley
Pricing Explained

Malt prices move every season, and sometimes sharply. For breweries, that can make procurement feel unpredictable, especially when prices change between annual contracts, spot purchases, and supplier quotations.

But malt pricing is not random. It follows a fairly clear chain: barley, crop quality, energy, processing cost, packaging, logistics, and demand from brewing and distilling. Once those parts are understood, it becomes much easier to read the market and make better purchasing decisions.

For most breweries, malt is not the largest line item in the business, but it is one of the most important. It affects recipe cost, production planning, working capital, and supplier relationships. A few pounds per tonne may look modest in isolation. Across repeated deliveries, multiple malt types, and a full brewing year, the difference becomes material.

Pricing starts with the harvest

Malt pricing begins with barley. In Europe, the key pricing period usually follows the summer harvest, with the market becoming clearer around August and September. In Australia, where the crop cycle is different, the comparable pricing window falls closer to February.

Once the harvest is complete, maltsters and grain merchants have much better visibility on yield, protein, germination, screening, moisture, and total availability. That is when the market begins to reset. Suppliers can see how much barley is suitable for malting, how much will be downgraded to feed, and what level of premium is needed to secure the right quality.

This is why post-harvest pricing matters so much. Even if malt prices continue to move during the year, the harvest sets the base. A good crop with strong quality usually creates more flexibility. A difficult crop, even if tonnage looks acceptable, can quickly tighten the malting barley market if protein is too high or screenings are poor.

The 2025 UK harvest showed this clearly. Winter barley performed better than many expected after a dry spring, but spring barley was more mixed, with drought contributing to higher nitrogen levels in some areas. For maltsters, that distinction matters. A crop can be large enough on paper but still produce a tighter pool of barley suitable for brewing malt.

Barley sets the direction, but quality sets the premium

At its core, malt is an agricultural product. Barley is the largest single input cost, so malt prices normally follow barley prices, though not always immediately and not always perfectly.

Feed barley is widely traded and relatively visible. Malting barley is different. It must meet tighter specifications, including approved varieties, germination, protein, moisture, and grain size. That creates a premium over feed barley.

The premium exists because malting barley is not simply "better barley". It is barley that meets a narrower technical standard. Brewers and maltsters need predictable extract, enzyme activity, germination, and processing performance. If the barley is too high in protein, too uneven, too small, or unsuitable for germination, it cannot be used for malt in the same way.

There is also a simple structural reason for the premium. Malting barley can be sold into the feed market if it fails specification or if demand is weak. Feed barley cannot simply be upgraded into malting barley. That one-way flexibility supports the price gap between the two.

The size of the premium changes with supply and demand. When good-quality malting barley is plentiful and demand from brewers, maltsters, and distillers is soft, the premium narrows. When suitable barley is scarce, the premium can widen quickly.

For brewers, the key point is that barley price alone does not tell the whole story. The quality of the barley crop matters just as much. A feed barley market may look comfortable, while malting barley remains tighter because the available crop does not meet brewing specification.

Demand from brewing and distilling can change the picture

Malt demand is not driven by beer alone. In the UK, distilling is also a major buyer of malting barley, particularly in Scotland. That means whisky production, export demand, and distillery utilisation all feed into the malt market.

This has been especially relevant recently. UK barley usage by brewers, maltsters, and distillers fell by 17% in the first half of the 2025/26 crop year, according to the Farm Advisory Service. That was described as the largest six-month decline since available records began in 1990, with weaker whisky export demand playing an important role.

When distilling demand is strong, it can support malting barley premiums even if beer volumes are flat. When distilling demand softens, the opposite can happen. That is one reason why malt prices do not always move in line with what brewers see in their own market.

Beer demand itself has also become more mature in many developed markets. Volumes in several regions have stabilised or softened, while growth has shifted into specific categories such as low-alcohol, alcohol-free, premium lager, craft, and export-led brands. For malt pricing, this means demand is not collapsing, but it is not necessarily creating the same broad upward pull seen in stronger growth cycles.

For procurement teams, the practical point is to watch the current demand from both brewing and distilling.

Energy, processing, and local cost structures

Malting is not only an agricultural process, but also an industrial process. Barley has to be steeped, germinated, kilned, cooled, handled, stored, cleaned, and packed under controlled conditions. That requires energy, labour, plant capacity, water, and technical control.

Kilning is particularly important. The final drying stage determines malt stability, colour, flavour development, and moisture. It also consumes significant energy. When gas and electricity prices rise, maltsters cannot simply absorb the increase indefinitely.

This is one reason malt prices can vary significantly from country to country, even within Europe. The UK is one of the more expensive malt markets, while countries such as France or Poland can be noticeably cheaper, despite not being especially far away geographically.

The difference is not just distance. It reflects structural cost differences: barley availability, energy prices, labour costs, plant scale, automation, local competition, domestic demand, and logistics. A modern maltings in a lower-cost region can operate on a very different base from an older or smaller facility in a higher-cost market.

Energy also affects barley before it reaches the maltings. Nitrogen fertiliser production is heavily linked to natural gas. When gas prices rise, fertiliser costs tend to follow, increasing the cost of growing barley.

That does not mean every movement in gas prices appears immediately in malt quotations. Farming decisions, fertiliser purchasing, crop cycles, and malt contracts all create a lag. But over a full season, higher energy and fertiliser costs usually work their way through the chain.

Format, handling, and crushing all add real cost

Once malt leaves the plant, the format becomes important. Bulk malt is the most efficient delivery method. It avoids bags, pallets, additional manual handling, and part-load inefficiencies. For larger breweries with silos, blowers, or bulk intake systems, this usually gives the lowest packaging cost per tonne.

Bagged malt is more flexible, but it is more expensive to produce and handle. Bags, pallets, wrapping, loading, storage, and additional warehouse movements all add cost. For smaller breweries, this is often unavoidable and perfectly rational. The point is not that bagged malt is inefficient, but that it carries a different cost structure.

Bulk delivery itself also has different cost levels. Tipper trucks are usually more cost-effective where the brewery has a suitable intake system. Blower trucks are more specialised and often more expensive, partly because availability is more limited. For breweries that need malt blown directly into silos, the convenience is valuable, but it should still be recognised as part of the delivered cost.

Pre-crushed malt adds another layer. The UK market is unusual in how commonly brewers buy malt already crushed. In many other brewing markets, milling in-house is the norm, even for relatively small breweries. In the UK, outsourcing the milling step is common because it saves time, space, equipment cost, and operational complexity.

That convenience has a price. Crushing requires equipment, labour, quality control, segregation, careful handling, and shorter practical shelf-life management. The cost is usually modest per tonne, but it is still real. For a brewery buying regularly, bagging and crushing together can make a meaningful difference compared with bulk whole malt.

This is where procurement decisions become operational decisions. A brewery does not always need the cheapest theoretical format. It needs the format that fits its intake, labour, cash flow, storage space, and production rhythm. But buyers should understand what they are paying for, especially when comparing quotations from different suppliers.

Logistics can move the final price more than expected

By the time malt is ready for dispatch, most of the cost has already been created. Logistics then determine how much more is added before the malt reaches the brewery.

For domestic UK supply, transport is driven mainly by distance, fuel, load size, and delivery method. A full articulated load into a brewery with suitable intake is very different from a small palletised delivery requiring more handling. The malt itself may be the same, but the delivered price will not be.

International logistics can be more counterintuitive. Sea freight is highly competitive, especially on full container load movements. In some cases, moving a full 40 ft container of malt from a port such as Gdansk to Yokohama in Japan or Santos in Brazil may cost only around USD 500 in ocean freight. Spread across a full container of malt, that can translate to roughly USD 17-20 per tonne.

That is the paradox of malt logistics. Long-distance international freight can sometimes be cheaper per tonne than domestic or regional road transport, provided the shipment is moved in full container loads and the route is competitive.

For imported malt, the full landed cost still needs to include more than ocean freight. Port charges, documentation, customs clearance, inland haulage, demurrage risk, and exchange rates all affect the final price. But the sea leg itself is often less expensive than many buyers assume.

This is one reason malt pricing is more local than many buyers expect. A surplus in one origin does not automatically mean cheap malt everywhere. If inland transport, port handling, or destination logistics are expensive, the saving at origin can disappear before the product reaches the brewery.

For UK brewers, this matters when comparing domestic, European, and non-European supply. The right comparison is not ex-works malt price. It is delivered cost, in the correct format, with realistic lead times and supply reliability included.

There is no simple malt index

Unlike wheat, oil, gas, or major traded commodities, malt does not have a single widely followed benchmark index. Barley markets are visible, but malt is more specialised. It sits between agriculture and brewing, with pricing shaped by variety, origin, specification, processing, format, and contract structure.

That makes the market more opaque. Two quotations can differ for good reasons: crop year, barley origin, malt type, bagged versus bulk, crushed versus whole, delivery distance, payment terms, supplier stock position, or contract length.

It also means relationships matter. A supplier with good access to origin, reliable logistics, and clear technical documentation may be more valuable than a supplier offering a slightly lower headline price but less certainty. For procurement managers, malt buying is not just a commodity exercise. It is a supply chain decision.

The best buyers therefore watch the market, but they also build structure. They understand when the harvest resets pricing, when to contract, when to hold stock, and when flexibility is worth paying for.

What brewers should watch

For all its moving parts, malt pricing can usually be read through a few core indicators.

The first is barley quality. A large harvest is not enough if the crop does not meet malting specification. Protein, germination, screenings, and variety approval all matter.

The second is the malting barley premium over feed barley. A narrow premium usually signals comfortable supply or weaker demand. A widening premium suggests tighter access to suitable barley.

The third is energy. Gas and electricity affect both malt production and fertiliser costs. When energy markets become volatile, malt prices rarely remain untouched.

The fourth is demand from brewing and distilling. Whisky demand, beer production, export markets, and maltster utilisation all influence how aggressively buyers compete for barley.

The fifth is logistics and format. Bagged, crushed, palletised, part-load, blower-delivered, tipper-delivered, and imported malt all carry costs that may be more controllable than the underlying barley market.

For breweries, the practical lesson is simple: do not look at malt price in isolation. Look at specification, format, delivery method, timing, and reliability. The cheapest tonne is not always the lowest-cost supply over a full brewing year.

Outlook for 2027

The 2027 malt outlook is likely to be shaped more by cost pressure than by a sudden jump in beer demand.

Energy is the main risk. Recent disruption around the Strait of Hormuz and the wider Middle East has pushed fuel, fertiliser, and electricity costs back into focus. If oil and gas prices remain elevated, the effect will eventually be felt across electricity, transport, fertiliser, and barley production costs.

That matters for barley. Barley is not the most input-intensive crop, but it still depends on fertiliser, fuel, machinery, and drying conditions. If farmers face higher input costs and weaker crop margins, planting decisions can change.

A smaller planted area does not automatically mean higher malt prices. Yield, weather, demand, imports, and quality will decide the final outcome. But it does reduce the margin for error. If a smaller crop is combined with quality problems or higher input costs, malting barley could tighten quickly.

For now, demand appears relatively steady rather than overheated. Beer volumes remain mature in many markets, and whisky demand has been softer after years of expansion. That should limit the demand-side pressure. The bigger risk is cost-side inflation: energy, fertiliser, logistics, and crop availability.

The net effect is clear enough. If energy and fertiliser markets remain elevated into the next growing cycle, malt prices are likely to face upward pressure.

For 2027, the breweries in the strongest position will be those that treat malt procurement as a managed process rather than a last-minute purchase. That means knowing annual requirements, understanding intake options, reviewing crushed versus whole malt economics, and securing supply in advance.
