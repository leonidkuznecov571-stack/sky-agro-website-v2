Pricing Explained

Malt prices move every season, sometimes sharply. For breweries, these shifts are not random. They follow a fairly clear logic, tied to agriculture, energy, and logistics. Understanding that logic makes it easier to time purchases and manage cost expectations.

Pricing starts with the harvest

Malt pricing is anchored to the barley harvest. In Europe, this typically concludes around September, while in Australia it falls closer to February. Once complete, suppliers have enough visibility to set prices for the season ahead. At that point, yield, quality, and available volumes are largely known, and the market resets.

While prices can move during the year, the post-harvest period remains the key reference point. It is when most contracts are shaped and expectations are set.

Barley sets the direction

At its core, malt is an agricultural product. Barley accounts for the largest share of production cost, and its price largely determines where malt will sit.

Feed barley is actively traded and widely tracked, which makes it a useful benchmark. Malting barley, however, is a stricter category. It requires specific varieties, lower protein, and tighter quality parameters. That alone places it at a premium.

There is also a structural asymmetry in the market. Malting barley can always be sold as feed if it fails to meet specification. The reverse is not true. Feed barley cannot simply be upgraded. That one-way flexibility underpins the price gap between the two.

In practice, this means malt prices tend to follow barley with a lag. A strong harvest brings relief across the board. A weak one tightens supply quickly.

Energy is not secondary

Malting is often thought of as a simple extension of agriculture. In reality, it is a controlled industrial process. Steeping, germination, and kilning all require precise temperature management and continuous airflow. That comes at a cost.

Electricity prices therefore feed directly into malt production. The impact varies widely depending on where the malt is produced. A modern plant in a low-cost energy region will operate on a very different cost base to an older facility in a high-cost market.

Over the past few years, this gap has become more visible, particularly as energy markets have fluctuated.

Labour and plant design

Labour plays a quieter role, but it is still part of the equation. The key difference is not geography alone, but plant design.

Older maltings require more manual input. Newer facilities are highly automated and can operate with significantly lower labour per tonne. As a result, two plants in similar regions can still have different cost structures.

For the buyer, this is rarely visible, but it is reflected in pricing consistency and competitiveness.

Format matters more than it seems

Once malt leaves the plant, the way it is packed and delivered starts to influence price in a more direct, mechanical way.

Bulk deliveries are the most efficient. Larger breweries receiving malt by tipper or blower avoid the cost of bags, pallets, and handling. Bagged formats, by contrast, introduce an additional layer of cost, typically around £20 per tonne once materials and handling are accounted for.

It is a simple difference, but one that becomes material over time, particularly for growing breweries.

The UK's preference for pre-crushed malt

One notable feature of the UK market is the widespread use of pre-crushed malt. In most other countries, breweries mill in-house as a standard part of the process. In the UK, many choose to outsource it.

That convenience comes at a cost, again typically in the region of £20 per tonne. It reflects the additional processing, handling, and quality control required to deliver ready-to-use malt.

It is not inherently inefficient, but it is a regional preference that shapes pricing in a way that is not always obvious when comparing markets.

Logistics: the final layer

By the time malt is ready, it has already accumulated most of its cost. Logistics then determine how much more is added before it reaches the brewery.

For domestic supply, the picture is relatively straightforward. Transport costs are driven largely by distance and fuel prices.

For import markets, the situation is different. Countries such as Brazil, Japan, or Vietnam rely heavily on imported malt. That introduces ocean freight, port handling, and inland distribution, each adding cost and variability.

Even within Europe, logistics can shift the final price more than expected, particularly when supply chains tighten.

A market without a clear index

Unlike many commodities, malt does not have a widely followed benchmark index. It sits in a narrower market, tied closely to brewing and distilling.

This makes pricing more opaque and more localised. Supply and demand are not just global, but regional. A surplus in one area does not always translate into lower prices elsewhere, especially when logistics are constrained.

For buyers, this means relationships and timing matter as much as raw market signals.

What to watch in practice

For all its moving parts, malt pricing can usually be read through a small number of indicators.

Barley quality and harvest outcomes remain the starting point. Energy costs follow closely behind, particularly in volatile markets. Beyond that, format and logistics are often the easiest levers for a brewery to control directly.

The rest is timing. Buying immediately post-harvest often provides clarity, if not always the lowest price. Waiting introduces uncertainty, but sometimes opportunity.

Outlook for 2027

Recent tensions around the Strait of Hormuz have brought energy markets back into focus. Any disruption or sustained risk premium in the region feeds directly into higher oil and gas prices, which in turn pushes up electricity costs across key malting regions.

At the same time, fertiliser prices tend to follow energy. Nitrogen fertilisers in particular are closely linked to natural gas. As input costs rise, barley production becomes more expensive, and that cost is eventually reflected in malt pricing.

Barley is not the most input-intensive crop, but it is still moderately dependent on fertiliser. When fertiliser prices move, barley follows, and malt follows shortly after.

Demand remains relatively stable. Beer volumes have levelled off in many markets, while whisky continues to provide longer-term support. The immediate pressure, however, is more likely to come from the cost side than from demand.

The net effect is straightforward: if energy markets remain elevated, expect upward pressure on malt prices into the next season.
