Brewing Water Chemistry: pH & Mineral Adjustment Guide

Water is the largest ingredient in any beer by volume, yet it is the one most brewers adjust least. Getting the mineral profile right — pH, calcium, sulphate, chloride — is one of the highest-leverage technical levers available. This guide explains the parameters that matter most and how to hit them consistently.

SKY AGRO Editorial · 10 March 2026 · 4 min read

Water chemistry has a direct effect on mash efficiency, fermentation performance, and finished-beer flavour balance.

In brewing terms, water chemistry affects every stage of production — from the mash, where pH determines enzyme activity and wort colour, through to the finished glass, where dissolved minerals shape bitterness perception and mouthfeel. Most municipal water supplies in the UK fall within a usable range for brewing, but few are ideal without some form of adjustment.

The practical challenge is that water chemistry is invisible — you cannot taste a high sulphate concentration in isolation, only its downstream effect on hop character. This makes it easy to deprioritise. Breweries that treat water as a variable rather than a constant tend to produce more consistent beer, especially when sourcing from multiple sites or blending mains water with RO-treated water.

Why water chemistry matters

An ideal mash pH sits between 5.2 and 5.4 — below this range, enzyme activity diminishes and conversion efficiency drops; above it, tannin and silicate extraction increases, contributing astringency and haze. Water hardness, specifically the concentration of calcium and magnesium ions, is the primary driver of natural mash pH depression.

Calcium also plays a structural role beyond pH: it promotes yeast flocculation, protects enzymes from heat denaturation, and reacts with oxalate to reduce gushing risk in finished beer. A minimum of 50 mg/L calcium is widely recommended; most brewing textbooks target 100–150 mg/L for well-attenuated, clean fermentations.

Key mineral parameters

pH

Mash pH should be measured with a calibrated probe at mash temperature, not at room temperature — pH readings shift with temperature and an uncorrected reading can mislead adjustments by 0.1–0.2 pH units. Lactic acid and phosphoric acid are the most commonly used acidulants; lactic at typical doses contributes a mild flavour that is detectable in very pale lagers, making phosphoric the preferred choice for light styles.

Sulphate and chloride

Higher sulphate relative to chloride (ratios above 2:1) emphasises hop dryness and bitterness — characteristic of Burton-style pale ales. Higher chloride (ratios below 0.5:1) rounds the palate and accentuates malt sweetness, which suits low-bitterness lagers and stouts. For balanced styles, targeting a ratio close to 1:1 with both ions in the 50–100 mg/L range is a sensible starting point.

Calcium additions are most commonly made using calcium sulphate (gypsum) or calcium chloride depending on the target profile. Calcium bicarbonate, present in hard water, counteracts acidulant additions and must be accounted for in calculations — either by pre-boiling to precipitate carbonates, or by diluting with RO or soft water before building a fresh mineral profile. For very soft water sources, building calcium levels from zero is often simpler than working around high-carbonate starting water.

The workflow for a typical brewery adjusting water per batch is: (1) measure source water minerals quarterly or when supplier changes occur; (2) define a target profile for each beer family — for example, one profile for pale ales and one for lagers; (3) calculate additions using a spreadsheet or dedicated water chemistry tool; (4) add mineral salts to the mash or kettle as appropriate; (5) confirm mash pH at 15 minutes and adjust with acidulant if needed.

A brewery that hits the same water profile reliably batch after batch will produce more consistent beer than one that aims for a theoretically optimal profile but applies it inconsistently. Record keeping — noting source water values, additions, and mash pH for every batch — pays dividends when diagnosing batch-to-batch variation.

Key points

  • Target mash pH 5.2–5.4, measured at mash temperature with a calibrated probe.
  • Minimum 50 mg/L calcium; 100–150 mg/L recommended for clean fermentations.
  • Sulphate-to-chloride ratio shapes bitterness vs. malt perception — adjust per style.
  • Lactic acid works for most styles; use phosphoric for very pale lagers where flavour impact matters.
  • High-carbonate water is easier to dilute with RO than to acid-neutralise at scale.
  • Measure source water quarterly — municipal profiles change seasonally.
