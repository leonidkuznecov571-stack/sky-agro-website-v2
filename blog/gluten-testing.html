Gluten Testing in Brewing:
Why It Matters

Gluten-free and gluten-reduced beers are increasingly common, but making reliable claims requires more than process changes. Accurate testing is essential, both for compliance and for consumer trust.

Where gluten comes from

Gluten is naturally present in barley and wheat, the core raw materials of most beers. During brewing, gluten proteins are partially broken down, but not fully removed.

This means standard beer cannot be assumed to be gluten-free without intervention.

Methods for reducing gluten

There are two main approaches to reducing gluten in beer: using alternative raw materials or breaking down gluten during processing.

The first involves brewing with naturally gluten-free grains such as sorghum, rice, or maize. This avoids the issue entirely, but changes the flavour profile.

The second approach uses enzymes to degrade gluten proteins during brewing.

Enzymatic gluten reduction

Specialised enzymes are added during fermentation to break down gluten into smaller fragments.

These enzymes:

  • Target specific protein structures
  • Work during fermentation and maturation
  • Allow breweries to use traditional barley-based recipes

This approach is widely used for "gluten-reduced" beers, as it preserves the expected flavour profile more effectively than alternative grains.

Why testing is still required

Even with enzymatic treatment, testing is essential.

Gluten breakdown is not always uniform, and residual fragments can remain. Regulatory thresholds, such as 20 ppm for gluten-free labelling in many regions, require verification.

Testing provides:

  • Compliance with labelling requirements
  • Consistency between batches
  • Protection against variability in raw materials and process

Common testing methods

The most widely used method is ELISA (Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay).

ELISA tests:

  • Detect specific gluten protein fragments
  • Are relatively quick and scalable
  • Are accepted for regulatory purposes

However, brewing introduces complexity. Hydrolysed gluten (broken-down proteins) can be harder to detect accurately, and different ELISA variants may give different results.

Limitations and interpretation

No test is perfect in brewing conditions.

Enzymatic treatment changes protein structure, which can affect detection. As a result, test results should be interpreted alongside process control, not in isolation.

Consistency in testing method is critical. Switching methods can lead to inconsistent results even if the process has not changed.

Practical approach for breweries

For most breweries, the key is combining process control with regular testing.

This means:

  • Using proven enzyme systems
  • Maintaining consistent dosing and conditions
  • Testing each batch or at defined intervals

The goal is not just passing a test once, but maintaining repeatable results over time.
