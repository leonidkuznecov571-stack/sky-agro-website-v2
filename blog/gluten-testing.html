Gluten Testing in Brewing:
Why It Matters

Gluten-free and gluten-reduced beers are increasingly common, but making reliable claims requires more than changing the recipe or adding an enzyme. For breweries, the issue is not only how gluten is reduced, but how the finished beer is tested, labelled, and controlled from batch to batch.

This matters because gluten is both a technical and consumer-safety issue. For some drinkers, gluten reduction is a preference. For people with coeliac disease, it is a medical requirement. That means breweries need to be precise with language, disciplined with testing, and careful not to overstate what the process can guarantee.

In the UK, only foods and drinks containing 20 parts per million of gluten or less can be labelled gluten-free. This threshold is widely used internationally, but beer creates specific testing challenges because brewing, fermentation, and enzyme treatment can break gluten proteins into smaller fragments. Those fragments may be harder to measure consistently than intact gluten in food.

Where gluten comes from

Gluten is naturally present in barley, wheat, rye, and related cereals. In brewing, the main source is usually malted barley, although wheat malt, raw wheat, rye, spelt, and other gluten-containing grains may also contribute.

Strictly speaking, barley does not contain "gluten" in exactly the same form as wheat. The relevant storage proteins are called prolamins. In barley, the key prolamins are hordeins. In wheat, they are gliadins and glutenins. In rye, they are secalins. In practical brewing language, these are usually grouped under gluten because they are the proteins relevant to coeliac disease and gluten labelling.

During mashing, boiling, fermentation, and maturation, many proteins are degraded, precipitated, adsorbed onto yeast, or removed with trub and finings. However, this does not mean standard beer can be assumed to be gluten-free. Some gluten-derived protein fragments can remain in the final product, especially when barley or wheat has been used as a core raw material.

That is why a brewery cannot rely on process assumptions alone. A beer may look bright, taste normal, and have gone through standard clarification, but still contain gluten above the relevant threshold. Testing is what turns a process claim into a verified product claim.

Gluten-free and gluten-reduced are not the same thing

There are two broad ways to make beers suitable for gluten-sensitive consumers. The first is to brew with naturally gluten-free raw materials. The second is to brew with traditional cereals such as barley, then reduce gluten through processing.

Naturally gluten-free beers avoid barley, wheat, and rye altogether. They may use grains or starch sources such as sorghum, rice, maize, millet, buckwheat, quinoa, or other gluten-free ingredients. From a technical standpoint, this is the cleanest route because the brewery is not trying to break down gluten after introducing it.

The challenge is flavour and brewhouse behaviour. Barley malt is central to beer because it provides extract, enzymes, colour, flavour, foam-positive proteins, and process familiarity. Replacing it changes the product. Some breweries use gluten-free grains very successfully, but the beer may need to be designed differently from the start.

The second route is gluten reduction. This usually means producing a barley-based beer and using process aids, most commonly enzymes, to reduce gluten-derived proteins below the required threshold. This allows the brewery to keep a more traditional beer profile, but it introduces analytical and labelling complexity.

For a UK brewer, the distinction must be handled carefully. A barley-based beer may be labelled gluten-free if it tests at 20 ppm or below, but the presence of barley still needs to be considered for allergen information and consumer communication. Coeliac UK notes that foods can only be labelled gluten-free at 20 ppm or less, and separate guidance recognises that beers made from barley may still need to declare barley as an allergen.

Enzymatic gluten reduction

Enzymatic gluten reduction is widely used because it allows breweries to work with traditional barley-based recipes while reducing gluten content in the finished beer.

These enzymes are usually added during fermentation or maturation. They break down specific protein structures into smaller fragments. In brewing, similar proteolytic activity can also help reduce chill haze, because haze is partly driven by interactions between proteins and polyphenols.

The main practical advantage is that the brewery can preserve much of the expected beer profile. The malt bill, brewhouse process, fermentation profile, and finished flavour can remain closer to a conventional beer than if the brewery had moved entirely to alternative grains.

The limitation is that enzyme treatment is not magic. It does not remove gluten in the same way that a filter removes particles. It breaks proteins into smaller pieces. That makes testing and interpretation more complicated, because the analytical method must detect hydrolysed gluten fragments rather than only intact proteins.

Process control is therefore critical. Enzyme dose, contact time, temperature, pH, fermentation conditions, beer style, original gravity, raw material variability, and clarification can all affect the final result. A brewery should not assume that because one batch tested below 20 ppm, every future batch will do the same automatically.

Why testing is still required

Testing is essential because gluten reduction must be verified in the finished product. This is especially true for breweries making gluten-free claims on beers produced from barley, wheat, or rye.

The legal and commercial risk is straightforward. If a beer is labelled gluten-free, the brewery must be confident that it meets the relevant threshold. In the UK, that means 20 ppm or less. A process description is not enough. A supplier statement about an enzyme is not enough. The finished beer needs appropriate testing.

Testing also protects consistency. Barley changes from season to season. Protein levels vary by crop year, variety, origin, and maltster. Brewhouse performance changes. Fermentation behaviour changes. Even small process shifts can affect residual protein fragments.

For breweries, gluten testing should therefore be treated like ABV testing, microbiological testing, or dissolved oxygen control. It is part of routine product assurance, not a one-off marketing exercise.

Testing is also important for consumer trust. Gluten-free beer customers are often highly label-conscious. If claims are vague, inconsistent, or poorly supported, confidence is lost quickly. Clear testing discipline gives the brewery a stronger basis for communication.

Common testing methods

The most common method used for gluten testing is ELISA, which stands for Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay. In simple terms, ELISA uses antibodies to detect specific gluten-related protein sequences.

For beer, the key issue is whether the gluten proteins are intact or hydrolysed. Standard foods may contain larger, more intact gluten proteins. Beer, especially fermented or enzyme-treated beer, often contains smaller fragments. That affects which test format is appropriate.

There are two main ELISA formats relevant here. Sandwich ELISA is generally used for intact gluten proteins because it requires two binding sites on the protein. Competitive ELISA is more suitable for hydrolysed or fermented products because it can detect smaller fragments where only one antibody binding site may remain.

This is why beer is usually treated differently from many solid foods. The Association of European Coeliac Societies states that R5 competitive ELISA is the current standard for detecting gluten in hydrolysed products, including beer, while also acknowledging that the method has limitations for very small gluten fragments formed during fermentation.

The European Brewery Convention also lists Method 9.51 for determining gluten in beer using the R5 Competitive ELISA method. This gives breweries a recognised analytical route, but it does not remove the need for careful interpretation.

Why beer is difficult to test

Beer is a difficult matrix for gluten testing because brewing changes the proteins before the laboratory ever receives the sample.

Mashing and fermentation begin the process of protein breakdown. Boiling and clarification remove some protein material. Enzyme treatment can then break gluten-derived proteins into even smaller fragments. The final product may therefore contain a mixture of peptides rather than intact gluten.

That matters because different tests can give different results. A study comparing gluten assessment methods in beer found that measured gluten values varied depending on the ELISA assay used, with R5 competitive ELISA detecting different amounts from other competitive and direct methods.

There is also ongoing scientific debate about whether current routine methods capture all clinically relevant fragments in hydrolysed barley-based beers. AOECS has noted that while R5 competitive ELISA is the current standard, it has limitations, and newer approaches such as LC-MS and multiplex ELISA are promising but not yet widely validated or accessible for routine use.

For brewers, the lesson is not that testing is pointless. The opposite is true. Testing is essential. But the results need to be interpreted with an understanding of the method, the matrix, and the product.

Limitations and interpretation

No gluten test is perfect in brewing conditions. The brewery needs to know what the chosen method is measuring, why it is appropriate, and how results will be interpreted over time.

The first limitation is hydrolysis. Enzyme-treated beers contain broken-down protein fragments. Some methods are better suited to detecting these than others. Using the wrong method can produce misleading results.

The second limitation is comparability. If a brewery changes laboratory, test kit, extraction method, or analytical format, results may shift even if the brewing process has not changed. This can create confusion if the brewery does not have a consistent testing protocol.

The third limitation is sampling. A result only represents the sample tested. If the tank is not homogeneous, if the sample is taken at the wrong stage, or if the beer changes after packaging, the test may not reflect the final product accurately.

The fourth limitation is interpretation near the threshold. A result close to 20 ppm should be treated carefully. Analytical uncertainty, batch variation, and process drift all matter. A brewery consistently testing at 18 or 19 ppm is in a very different position from one consistently testing far below the threshold.

This is where quality systems become important. A brewery should not rely on a single passing result. It should build evidence across repeated batches, using the same method, the same sampling point, and a clear internal standard for release.

Practical approach for breweries

For most breweries, the best approach is to combine process control with regular laboratory testing.

The first step is deciding what kind of beer is being produced. If the aim is a naturally gluten-free beer, the raw material strategy must exclude gluten-containing cereals and control cross-contamination. If the aim is a barley-based gluten-reduced or gluten-free-labelled beer, the brewery needs a validated enzyme process and a suitable testing regime.

The second step is controlling the process. Enzyme dosing should be measured accurately and recorded. Fermentation and maturation conditions should be consistent. Contact time should be sufficient. Raw material changes should be documented. Any process change should trigger extra testing until the brewery has confidence in the new baseline.

The third step is choosing the right test. For hydrolysed beers, this usually means using a laboratory familiar with beer and an appropriate competitive ELISA method. The brewery should confirm the method used, the reporting limit, the units, and whether the lab has experience with fermented and enzyme-treated products.

The fourth step is building a testing rhythm. Some breweries may test every batch, especially where gluten-free claims are central to the product. Others may test at defined intervals once the process is mature, with additional testing after recipe, malt, enzyme, or process changes. The right frequency depends on risk, scale, and claim.

The fifth step is label discipline. The wording must reflect the product and the jurisdiction. "Gluten-free", "gluten-reduced", and "brewed to remove gluten" are not interchangeable phrases. Breweries should also think carefully about allergen declarations where barley or wheat has been used.

What brewers should watch

Gluten testing is not just a compliance detail. It is part of making a reliable product.

The first point to watch is the raw material base. A beer brewed from naturally gluten-free grains is fundamentally different from a barley-based beer treated with enzymes. Both can have a place in the market, but they should not be presented as the same process.

The second is enzyme control. Enzymatic reduction can work well, but only when dosing, timing, and process conditions are consistent. It should not be treated as an informal addition to the tank.

The third is the testing method. Beer usually requires methods suitable for hydrolysed gluten. Competitive R5 ELISA is widely recognised for this purpose, but breweries should understand its limitations and keep the method consistent.

The fourth is batch evidence. One passing test is not the same as a controlled product. Repeatability matters more than a single result.

The fifth is communication. Gluten-related claims are sensitive because they affect consumer health and trust. The best approach is clear, conservative, and evidence-led.

For breweries, the practical lesson is simple: gluten-free or gluten-reduced beer should be treated as a controlled production category, not just a recipe variation. The strongest products are built around raw material control, validated enzyme use, appropriate testing, and consistent labelling.
