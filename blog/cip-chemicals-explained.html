CIP Chemicals Explained
What to Use, When

Cleaning-in-place, or CIP, is central to brewery hygiene. It allows tanks, pipework, heat exchangers, fillers, hoses, and process equipment to be cleaned without dismantling everything after each production cycle.

But effective CIP is not just a case of circulating a strong chemical and hoping for the best. The right product has to be used at the right concentration, temperature, flow rate, and contact time. If one part of that balance is wrong, the result can be wasted chemical, longer turnaround times, poor hygiene, damaged equipment, or product instability.

For breweries, the practical objective is simple: remove organic soil, control mineral deposits, sanitise clean surfaces before use, and make the process repeatable enough that every production cycle starts from a known hygienic baseline.

Cleaning versus sanitising

Cleaning and sanitising are not the same thing.

Cleaning removes soil. In a brewery, that means yeast, protein, sugars, hop residues, carbohydrate deposits, biofilms, colour residues, mineral scale, and beer stone.

Sanitising, sometimes described as disinfection in wider food-hygiene language, reduces microorganisms on already-clean surfaces to an acceptable level.

A sanitiser cannot reliably compensate for poor cleaning. If organic soil or mineral deposits remain on a surface, they can protect microorganisms and reduce chemical effectiveness. This is why a CIP programme is normally built as a sequence rather than a single chemical wash.

A typical CIP sequence includes:

Pre-rinse to remove loose soil and warm the circuit.

Caustic wash to remove organic residues.

Intermediate rinse to remove caustic, alkalinity, and suspended soil.

Acid wash where mineral scale or beer stone needs control.

Final rinse or terminal sanitisation, depending on the process and chemical used.

The exact cycle depends on the equipment, soil load, water quality, product, and hygiene risk. A fermenter, brewhouse vessel, keg line, filler, and pasteuriser do not all need the same programme.

Four CIP variables

CIP performance depends on four linked variables: chemistry, temperature, time, and mechanical action. This is often described as the TACT principle or Sinner's Circle. If one factor is reduced, another usually has to increase to compensate.

Chemistry is the detergent or sanitiser used. Caustic, acid, peracetic acid, chlorine-based products, peroxide systems, and enzymatic cleaners all do different jobs.

Temperature affects cleaning speed and soil removal. Hot caustic is normally more effective against brewery organic soils than the same solution used cold, provided the temperature is suitable for the equipment, materials, and chemical formulation.

Time is contact time. The solution must stay in contact with all product-contact surfaces long enough to work.

Mechanical action is flow, turbulence, spray-ball coverage, impingement, and physical contact. In a CIP circuit, poor flow can make the right chemical ineffective. Dead legs, blocked spray balls, low pump performance, air locks, and badly designed pipework can all leave areas under-cleaned.

This is why changing chemical is not always the answer. Many CIP failures come from poor coverage, low temperature, short contact time, incorrect concentration, or weak flow rather than from the product itself.

Caustic cleaning

Caustic cleaning is the backbone of most brewery CIP programmes. Sodium hydroxide-based cleaners are used because they are highly effective against organic soils, including yeast residues, protein films, hop compounds, sugars, and general brewery deposits.

In practice, caustic is usually circulated warm or hot, depending on the equipment and supplier instructions. Temperature improves cleaning efficiency, especially where soils are sticky, proteinaceous, heat-affected, or dried on. Concentration, temperature, and contact time should be controlled rather than guessed.

Plain caustic soda can work, but many brewery caustics are formulated. They may include surfactants, sequestrants, chelating agents, dispersants, or wetting agents. These additives help the solution penetrate soils, suspend residues, reduce redeposition, control foam, and maintain performance in harder water.

Hard water matters. Calcium and magnesium can interfere with cleaning performance and contribute to scale formation. In hard-water areas, reinforced or formulated caustics are often more reliable than simple sodium hydroxide. This is one reason two breweries using the same caustic concentration can see different results.

Caustic is also used in draught line and cellar hygiene. Caustic-based line cleaners are common because they break down biofilms and beer-spoiling residues, although handling precautions are essential.

The key point is that caustic is a cleaner, not a complete hygiene programme by itself. It removes the organic layer that would otherwise protect microorganisms or trap mineral deposits. It normally needs to be followed by proper rinsing and, where required, acid cleaning or terminal sanitisation.

Rinsing matters

Rinsing looks simple, but it is one of the most important parts of the cycle. After a caustic wash, the rinse removes spent cleaning solution, dissolved soil, suspended particles, and alkalinity from the circuit.

If rinsing is poor, several things can happen. Caustic residue can carry into the acid stage, neutralising the acid and reducing its effectiveness. Detergent residue can remain on product-contact surfaces. Soil can be redeposited. Chemical carryover can create safety risks, flavour issues, or equipment damage.

The same applies between acid cleaning and sanitising. Acid and caustic should not be mixed in the same line, vessel, or bucket. Systems should be flushed until the previous chemical has been removed before the next stage begins.

In a brewery CIP programme, rinse control should be practical and measurable. Operators should not rely only on time. Conductivity, pH, visual clarity, temperature, and defined rinse volumes can all help confirm that the previous stage has been properly removed.

Rinse water quality also matters. If final rinse water is microbiologically poor, it can recontaminate cleaned equipment. This is one reason many breweries use a terminal sanitiser such as peracetic acid immediately before use, especially on cold-side equipment.

Acid cleaning

Acid cleaning has a different role from caustic cleaning. It is used to remove inorganic deposits, water scale, and beer stone. In breweries, beer stone is mainly calcium oxalate, often combined with protein and other residues. It can build up on tanks, pipework, heat exchangers, filters, and fittings.

Beer stone matters because it creates rough surfaces and harbourage points. Once deposits form, they can protect microorganisms and make routine caustic cleaning less effective.

Acid cleaners commonly use phosphoric acid, nitric acid, or nitric/phosphoric blends. These products are used to remove mineral deposits and maintain clean stainless product-contact surfaces when used correctly. Some nitric-containing programmes may also support stainless surface condition, but passivation should be treated as a separate controlled procedure where required.

Acid is not necessarily required in every CIP cycle. Its frequency depends on water hardness, beer style, equipment use, heat load, surface condition, and observed deposit formation. A brewhouse vessel or heat exchanger may need acid treatment more frequently than a tank that is already cleaning well with caustic and sanitiser.

The key is not to treat acid cleaning as optional forever. If beer stone is allowed to build up, cleaning becomes progressively harder. Preventative acid cycles are usually easier and cheaper than trying to recover heavily fouled equipment later.

Peracetic acid

Once equipment is clean, it may need to be sanitised before use. In breweries, peracetic acid, usually shortened to PAA, is one of the most common terminal sanitisers.

PAA is popular because it acts quickly, works at low temperatures, and breaks down into acetic acid, oxygen, and water. It is widely used for tanks, pipework, hoses, filling equipment, and other product-contact surfaces.

PAA is often described as a no-rinse sanitiser. That point needs careful handling. No-rinse does not mean no-drain, no-dilution control, or no contact-time control. It only applies when the product is used at the correct concentration, for the correct contact time, on already-clean surfaces, and in accordance with supplier instructions.

More is not better. Excess PAA can create safety risks, affect flavour, increase corrosion risk, and damage sensitive materials.

PAA is a sanitiser, not a cleaner. It should not be expected to remove heavy soil, yeast build-up, hop residues, or beer stone. If it is applied to a dirty surface, performance will be compromised.

For cold-side brewery work, PAA is often used immediately before filling or transfer. The vessel or line should already have been cleaned, rinsed, and drained. The PAA stage then reduces microbial risk before beer enters the system.

Hydrogen peroxide

Hydrogen peroxide appears in brewery hygiene in two main ways. It can be used as part of oxidising cleaning systems, and it can also be present in sanitising blends.

On its own, hydrogen peroxide is an oxidising agent. It can help break down certain organic residues and support cleaning under the right conditions. In alkaline cleaning, peroxide or other oxidising additives may help attack stubborn organic soils, colour residues, or proteinaceous films.

Mineral scale and beer stone, however, normally still require an acid cleaning strategy. Peroxide should not be treated as a universal replacement for caustic, acid, or PAA.

Its performance depends heavily on concentration, temperature, soil load, pH, contact time, and formulation. It is best understood as part of a specific cleaning chemistry, not as a generic cure-all.

Mixed oxidising systems can be very effective, but they should be used with discipline. Compatibility, dosing, ventilation, PPE, and supplier instructions matter. Oxidisers can react strongly with other chemicals, especially if products are mixed incorrectly.

Chlorine products

Chlorine-based products, including sodium hypochlorite and chlorinated alkaline cleaners, can be effective against a broad range of microorganisms and certain organic soils. They are sometimes used for targeted cleaning, specific contamination problems, or open-plant cleaning applications.

In routine brewery CIP, however, chlorine chemistry needs caution. Chlorine-based products can be corrosive to stainless steel if used incorrectly, especially at high concentration, high temperature, low pH, or extended contact time. They can also create flavour taints if residues remain.

That does not mean chlorine products have no place. They can be useful where the application is appropriate, the material compatibility is understood, and rinsing is controlled. But they are normally more targeted than PAA in a modern brewery hygiene programme, especially on stainless product-contact surfaces.

The most important rule is never to mix chlorine products with acids. Acidifying hypochlorite can release chlorine gas. This is a serious safety hazard, not just a process mistake.

Enzymatic cleaners

Enzymatic cleaners are increasingly used for targeted brewery applications. They use enzymes such as proteases, amylases, or other specialised activities to break down specific soils under milder conditions.

They can be useful where protein build-up, biofilms, starch residues, or complex organic deposits are difficult to remove with standard chemistry. They may also suit certain sensitive materials or lower-temperature applications where aggressive caustic cleaning is not ideal.

The strength of enzymatic cleaners is specificity. They can break down certain residues very effectively when the conditions are right. The limitation is that enzymes are sensitive to temperature, pH, contact time, and chemical compatibility.

A protease-based cleaner will not perform properly if the process conditions denature the enzyme. An enzymatic product will also not solve a problem caused mainly by mineral scale, poor flow, blocked spray devices, or damaged surfaces.

For breweries, enzymatic cleaners are best used with a clear purpose. They are not a default replacement for a well-designed caustic, acid, and sanitising programme. They are a useful tool when the soil type and equipment justify them.

Match soil first

The simplest way to choose CIP chemistry is to start with the soil.

CIP chemistry by soil type and hygiene risk
Soil or risk Main chemistry Practical note
Yeast and protein Caustic or alkaline cleaner Core brewery cleaning step
Hop residues Formulated caustic Surfactants may improve removal
Sugars and carbohydrates Alkaline cleaner Temperature and flow matter
Beer stone Acid cleaner Usually calcium oxalate-based
Water scale Acid cleaner Frequency depends on water hardness
Biofilms Combined strategy Cleaning, flow, inspection, and sanitising all matter
Microbial risk PAA or approved sanitiser Use only on clean surfaces
Stubborn organic films Oxidising or enzymatic support Use for defined problems
Chlorine-sensitive areas Avoid or tightly control chlorine Never mix with acids

Organic soils need alkaline cleaning. Yeast residues, protein films, hop resins, sugars, carbohydrates, and general beer soils are normally handled with caustic or formulated alkaline cleaners.

Mineral soils need acid cleaning. Beer stone, water scale, and inorganic deposits are handled with acid cleaners.

Microbial control needs sanitising. Once surfaces are clean, products such as PAA are used to reduce microbial load before the next production step.

Biofilms usually need a combined strategy. Caustic may help break down the organic matrix, acid may help remove mineral components, and a terminal sanitiser may be needed after the surface is properly cleaned. Where biofilms are established, mechanical action, temperature, inspection, and extended contact time may be just as important as chemical choice.

"Stronger chemical" is often the wrong response. If the problem is mineral scale, more caustic may not solve it. If the problem is organic soil, acid alone will not clean it properly. If the problem is poor spray-ball coverage, changing chemical may only hide the issue temporarily.

Brewery area programmes

Different areas of the brewery need different cleaning priorities.

Brewhouse vessels usually see heavy organic loading, heat-affected soils, protein deposits, hop residues, and scale. Hot caustic is normally central here, with acid cleaning used as needed to control mineral deposits and beer stone.

Fermenters and conditioning tanks need strong organic soil removal, especially yeast and protein residues, followed by sanitising before use. Acid cycles may be periodic rather than every time, depending on beer stone formation, water quality, and inspection results.

Bright beer tanks, transfer lines, and fillers are more sensitive because they sit close to packaging. Cleaning must be effective, but oxygen pickup, rinse quality, microbial control, and final sanitising are especially important.

Heat exchangers require particular attention because narrow channels and high surface area can trap soil and scale. Caustic and acid cycles are both important, and flow rate must be sufficient to reach effective cleaning conditions.

Kegging and packaging lines need disciplined cleaning and sanitising because contamination at this point can affect finished beer directly. Valves, heads, fillers, seals, and short pipe runs should be treated as high-risk areas.

Hoses, gaskets, valves, and fittings are common weak points. They may be included in CIP circuits, but they still need inspection and periodic replacement. Chemical cleaning cannot fully compensate for worn seals, damaged surfaces, crevices, or badly designed dead legs.

Material compatibility

CIP chemistry does not only contact stainless steel. It also contacts gaskets, pump seals, valve seats, hoses, sight glasses, elastomers, plastics, and sometimes soft metals.

These materials do not all tolerate chemicals in the same way. Over-strength caustic, hot alkaline washes, oxidisers, chlorine products, strong acids, or extended PAA exposure can shorten gasket life, swell elastomers, embrittle plastics, or damage surfaces.

Material compatibility should be checked against chemical supplier guidance and equipment manufacturer recommendations. This is especially important for flexible hoses, soft seals, dosing equipment, cellar fittings, and packaging components.

If seals are cracking, swelling, hardening, discolouring, or shedding particles, the problem may not be the cleaning result. It may be chemical exposure, temperature, age, or incompatible material selection.

Common CIP mistakes

Many CIP problems come from small routine errors rather than complete system failure.

One common mistake is under-dosing chemical. Operators may reduce concentration to save cost, but then need longer cycles, higher temperatures, or repeat cleaning. The saving is often false.

Another is over-dosing chemical. This increases cost, creates rinse problems, raises safety risks, and may damage materials without improving cleaning.

A third is poor temperature control. If caustic is designed to run hot but is circulated too cool, soil removal may be incomplete. If temperature is too high for the product or material, equipment damage can result.

A fourth is weak mechanical action. Blocked spray balls, low pump flow, poor return, air locking, and dead legs all reduce cleaning performance. The chemistry may be correct, but the surface still does not see enough cleaning action.

A fifth is skipping acid cycles until deposits are visible. By the time beer stone can be seen, the surface may already be harder to clean. Preventative acid cleaning is usually more effective than corrective cleaning.

A sixth is treating sanitising as cleaning. PAA on a dirty surface is poor practice. The surface must be cleaned first.

A seventh is relying on "the usual cycle" without verification. CIP must be measured, recorded, and challenged. Habit is not control.

Monitoring and verification

A CIP programme should be repeatable and measurable. At minimum, breweries should record chemical concentration, temperature, contact time, flow or pump settings, and whether the cycle completed correctly.

Verification does not need to be complicated, but it does need to exist.

Conductivity can confirm chemical presence, phase changes, and rinse completion. pH can help confirm neutralisation. Visual inspection can identify obvious soil, beer stone, or poor drainage. ATP testing, swabbing, or microbiological testing can help check hygiene performance. Spray-ball tests can confirm coverage. Trend data can show whether a problem is developing before it becomes visible.

Conductivity is useful, but it does not always prove active chemical strength, especially with formulated detergents or reused cleaning solutions. Active concentration should be checked periodically by titration, test kits, or supplier-approved methods.

The point is to move from habit to control. A brewery that knows its CIP parameters can troubleshoot properly. A brewery that only knows "we ran the usual cycle" has much less evidence when a contamination or quality issue appears.

Red flags

Stop and investigate if rinse water remains cloudy, spray balls are not rotating, return flow is weak, caustic temperature is below target, chemical concentration is drifting, PAA is being used on visibly soiled surfaces, beer stone is visible, ATP or microbiology results are trending upward, or gaskets are swelling, cracking, or shedding.

These signs usually indicate a process issue, not just a chemical issue. Increasing concentration may not fix the root cause.

What brewers should watch

CIP is not about using as many chemicals as possible. It is about using the right chemistry in the right sequence, under controlled conditions.

The first point to watch is soil type. Organic residues need alkaline cleaning. Mineral deposits need acid. Clean surfaces need sanitising.

The second is water quality. Hard water changes the way caustics perform and increases the risk of scale. In hard water areas, formulated products and acid cycles become more important.

The third is mechanical action. If flow, spray coverage, or pipework design is poor, chemistry alone will not fix the problem.

The fourth is chemical separation. Caustic, acid, chlorine products, and oxidisers must be handled correctly and never mixed casually. Rinsing between stages is both a hygiene issue and a safety issue.

The fifth is verification. Good CIP is not just a written procedure. It is a controlled process with records, checks, and corrective action.

For breweries, reliable CIP comes from sequence and control, not chemical strength alone. Caustic removes the organic load. Acid controls mineral scale and beer stone. PAA or another approved terminal sanitiser prepares clean surfaces for use. Rinsing, flow, temperature, contact time, and verification decide whether those chemicals actually reach and clean the surfaces that matter.
