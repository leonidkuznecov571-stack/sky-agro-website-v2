How Non-Alcoholic
& Low ABV Beer Is Made

Low- and non-alcoholic beers have moved from niche to mainstream. For breweries, producing them consistently requires more than simply "removing alcohol". The method chosen affects flavour, body, and process complexity.

Two fundamentally different approaches

There are two main ways to produce low- and non-alcoholic beer: limiting alcohol formation during fermentation, or removing alcohol after fermentation.

The first approach focuses on controlling yeast activity. The second treats alcohol removal as a downstream processing step. Each comes with trade-offs.

Limiting alcohol during fermentation

One method is to restrict fermentation so that less alcohol is produced in the first place. This can be done through:

  • Specialised low-attenuation yeast strains
  • Lower fermentation temperatures
  • High mash temperatures to produce less fermentable wort

The advantage is simplicity. No additional equipment is required, and the process integrates easily into a standard brewhouse.

The limitation is flavour. Reduced fermentation often leads to wort-like notes, lower complexity, and thinner profiles. Maintaining balance becomes the main challenge.

Arrested fermentation

A variation of this approach is stopping fermentation early.

Fermentation begins normally, allowing some flavour development, but is halted before alcohol rises too far. This is typically done through cooling or filtration.

It improves flavour compared to fully restricted fermentation, but requires precise control. Timing is critical, and consistency can be difficult at scale.

Dealcoholisation after fermentation

The second approach is to produce a full-strength beer and remove the alcohol afterwards.

This is where specialised equipment comes in, most commonly:

  • Vacuum distillation
  • Membrane filtration (reverse osmosis)

These systems separate alcohol from beer while aiming to retain aroma and flavour compounds.

Vacuum distillation

Under vacuum, alcohol boils at a lower temperature. This allows it to be removed with less thermal damage to the beer.

Modern systems are designed to:

  • Preserve volatile aroma compounds
  • Maintain mouthfeel
  • Reduce "cooked" flavours

It is a reliable and widely used method, particularly for larger breweries.

Membrane systems

Membrane filtration separates alcohol through pressure rather than heat.

Beer is passed through membranes that allow alcohol and water to pass while retaining larger flavour molecules. The alcohol is then removed, and the remaining liquid is recombined.

This approach can deliver very high-quality results, but is more complex to operate and maintain.

Blending and flavour adjustment

In practice, many breweries combine methods.

A dealcoholised base may be blended with a small portion of full-strength beer, or adjusted with:

  • Natural flavours
  • Sugars
  • Carbonation changes

This final step is often what defines the quality of the finished product.

Practical considerations

Choosing a method depends on scale, positioning, and available equipment.

Smaller breweries often start with fermentation control due to lower capital requirements. Larger operations tend to invest in dealcoholisation to achieve closer parity with standard beer.

Consistency, rather than absolute alcohol level, is usually the main operational challenge.
