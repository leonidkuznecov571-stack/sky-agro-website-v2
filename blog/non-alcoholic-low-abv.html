How Low- and
Non-Alcoholic Beer Is Made

Low- and non-alcoholic beers have moved from niche to mainstream. For breweries, they are no longer just a January product or a small fridge option for non-drinkers. They are part of a broader moderation trend, with UK no- and low-alcohol beer sales reported to have reached record levels in 2025.

Producing them consistently, however, is not as simple as "removing alcohol". Alcohol contributes more than strength. It affects aroma, flavour release, mouthfeel, sweetness perception, bitterness balance, body, and microbial stability. Once alcohol is reduced or removed, the beer behaves differently.

For brewers and procurement teams, the key question is therefore not just whether a product can be made below a certain ABV. It is which production method gives the right balance of flavour quality, process control, capital cost, and repeatability.

Start with the definition

In the UK, the terms used on low- and non-alcoholic beer are not purely marketing language. They sit within specific guidance. "Alcohol-free" is generally used for drinks not exceeding 0.05% ABV, while "low alcohol" refers to drinks not exceeding 1.2% ABV. "De-alcoholised" is used where alcohol has been extracted from the drink and the final product does not exceed 0.5% ABV.

This matters because the target ABV affects the process. A beer designed to finish below 1.2% ABV gives the brewer more flexibility than one that must reach 0.5% or 0.05%. The lower the target, the less margin there is for fermentation drift, measurement variation, and post-packaging refermentation.

It also affects positioning. A 1.0% table beer, a 0.5% de-alcoholised lager, and a 0.05% alcohol-free pale ale are not the same technical challenge. They may sit close together commercially, but the production requirements are very different.

For that reason, the first decision is not equipment. It is specification. Before choosing a method, the brewery needs to know the required ABV, flavour target, style, shelf-life, packaging format, route to market, and level of production risk it is willing to manage.

Two fundamentally different approaches

There are two main ways to produce low- and non-alcoholic beer: limit alcohol formation during fermentation, or produce a more conventional beer and remove alcohol afterwards. Reviews of alcohol-free beer production generally group the methods into these two categories: biological methods, which control or restrict fermentation, and physical methods, which remove ethanol after fermentation.

The first approach works upstream. The brewer designs the wort and fermentation so that less alcohol is produced in the first place. This may involve lower fermentability, specialised yeast, cold contact fermentation, arrested fermentation, or a combination of these controls.

The second approach works downstream. The brewery produces a beer closer to standard strength, then removes alcohol using equipment such as vacuum distillation or membrane filtration. This normally gives the brewer more conventional fermentation character, but introduces additional capital cost, utilities, product handling, and process complexity.

Neither route is automatically better. Fermentation-limited beers can be simpler and cheaper to produce, but are often harder to make taste like a fully fermented beer. Dealcoholised beers can achieve a more familiar flavour profile, but require more investment and tighter process management.

Limiting alcohol during fermentation

The simplest route is to restrict how much alcohol is produced. This usually starts in the brewhouse. High mash temperatures can increase the proportion of unfermentable sugars in the wort, leaving less fermentable extract available for yeast. The brewer can also adjust original gravity, malt bill, yeast selection, fermentation temperature, and fermentation time.

Specialised low-attenuation yeasts are another option. These strains ferment a narrower range of sugars, allowing the brewery to generate some fermentation character without converting as much extract into alcohol. Used properly, they can help build ester profile and beer-like aroma while keeping ABV low.

The advantage is that the process can often be run on existing brewhouse and cellar equipment. For smaller breweries, this is the main appeal. There is no need to invest immediately in dealcoholisation equipment, and the production process is easier to integrate into normal operations.

The limitation is flavour. Reduced fermentation often leaves wort-like sweetness, cereal notes, lower ester complexity, and a thinner profile. Alcohol also gives beer warmth and body, so removing it from the equation changes the balance of bitterness, sweetness, carbonation, and aroma. The beer may be technically correct, but still taste unfinished.

Arrested fermentation and cold contact methods

Arrested fermentation is a more controlled version of the same idea. Fermentation begins, allowing some yeast-derived flavour development, but is stopped before the alcohol rises too far. This may be done through rapid cooling, filtration, centrifugation, pasteurisation, or another stabilisation step.

The benefit is that the beer can develop more fermentation character than a product where fermentation is heavily restricted from the beginning. The brewer gives the yeast some time to work, then stops the process before the ABV exceeds the target.

The difficulty is control. Timing matters. A few hours of extra fermentation can be enough to push the beer outside specification, especially at very low ABV targets. The process must also prevent refermentation later, particularly if residual fermentable sugars remain in the beer.

Cold contact fermentation follows similar logic. Yeast is brought into contact with wort at low temperature, slowing metabolism and limiting alcohol formation while still allowing some flavour-active compounds to develop. It can be useful, but it requires discipline. Yeast handling, residence time, temperature, sanitation, and analytical control all become critical.

For breweries using fermentation-control methods, microbiological stability is one of the main concerns. Low- and non-alcoholic beers often contain more residual extract and less alcohol, which means they can be less inherently stable than standard beer. Brewers Association guidance specifically highlights quality control, pasteurisation, and packaging safety as key issues in alcohol-free beer production.

Dealcoholisation after fermentation

The second main route is to brew a standard or near-standard beer first, then remove the alcohol afterwards. For many breweries, this is the route that gives the most convincing flavour, because the beer has gone through a fuller fermentation before alcohol is removed.

This matters because fermentation creates much of what consumers recognise as beer character. Yeast produces esters, higher alcohols, sulphur compounds, organic acids, and other flavour-active components. A beer that has never fermented properly can struggle to achieve the same depth.

Dealcoholisation therefore starts from a stronger base. The brewer can make a beer with more conventional fermentation character, then reduce the alcohol to the required level. The challenge is to remove ethanol without stripping too much aroma, damaging mouthfeel, or creating cooked flavours.

The most common physical approaches are vacuum distillation and membrane-based separation, including reverse osmosis. Both are established methods, and both can produce high-quality results when properly designed and operated. Reviews of alcohol-free beer production identify vacuum-based evaporation and membrane processes as major technologies used for ethanol removal.

Vacuum distillation

Vacuum distillation removes alcohol by lowering the pressure around the beer. Under reduced pressure, ethanol evaporates at a lower temperature than it would under normal atmospheric conditions. This allows alcohol to be removed with less thermal stress than conventional boiling.

For beer, this is important. Aroma compounds are delicate, and heat can quickly make a product taste flat, cooked, or stale. Modern vacuum systems are designed to reduce this damage by operating at lower temperatures and by managing volatile aroma recovery more carefully.

The strength of vacuum distillation is reliability. It is a well-understood industrial process and can be scaled for consistent production. For breweries producing larger volumes of lager, pale ale, or other mainstream styles, it can be a strong option because it gives clear ABV control and repeatable results.

The trade-off is capital cost and process design. The equipment requires space, utilities, cleaning, trained operators, and integration with the wider brewery. Product handling also becomes more complex because the beer is processed after fermentation and before final adjustment or packaging.

Vacuum distillation is particularly relevant where a brewery wants a product that tastes closer to standard beer, but still needs a controlled low or alcohol-free specification. It is not a shortcut. It is a dedicated production step.

Membrane systems

Membrane systems take a different approach. Rather than using heat, they separate alcohol through selective membranes, pressure, and concentration gradients. Reverse osmosis is the best-known example, although other membrane processes such as dialysis, osmotic distillation, and pervaporation are also discussed in technical literature.

In simple terms, beer is passed across a membrane that allows smaller molecules such as alcohol and water to pass through, while retaining larger flavour and colour compounds in the beer stream. The alcohol is then removed from the permeate, and water may be recombined to bring the product back into balance.

The main advantage is flavour protection. Because the process does not rely primarily on heat, it can be gentle on certain aroma and flavour compounds. This makes membrane technology attractive for breweries that want to preserve a fresh beer profile.

The challenge is operating complexity. Membranes must be managed, cleaned, monitored, and replaced over time. Flow rate, pressure, temperature, fouling, oxygen pickup, and hygiene all affect performance. The process is not simply a filter installed at the end of the line.

For smaller breweries, membrane systems can be attractive in theory but demanding in practice. They require technical confidence and careful cost modelling. For larger breweries, the higher operational complexity may be justified by product quality and brand positioning.

Blending, body, and flavour adjustment

In practice, the best low- and non-alcoholic beers are often not defined by the alcohol removal step alone. They are defined by what happens afterwards.

Once alcohol is reduced, the beer usually needs rebalancing. Bitterness can seem sharper. Sweetness can become more obvious. Hop aroma may seem thinner. Body can fall away. Carbonation may need to be adjusted to create freshness and lift. The final product has to be built back into balance.

Breweries may use blending, natural flavour adjustment, dry hopping, malt extract, unfermented wort, sugars, stabilisers, or carbonation changes, depending on style and regulatory requirements. A small amount of full-strength beer may also be blended in some products, provided the final ABV remains within specification and the labelling is correct.

Mouthfeel is particularly important. Alcohol gives beer body and helps carry flavour. Without it, the product can seem watery even if the recipe looks sensible on paper. Malt selection, mash profile, residual extract, carbonation, chloride-to-sulphate balance, and finishing process all become more important.

This is where low- and non-alcoholic brewing becomes closer to product development than simple brewing. The process does not end when the target ABV is reached. It ends when the beer tastes complete.

Stability and packaging are critical

Low- and non-alcoholic beer carries a different stability risk from standard beer. With lower alcohol, higher residual sugars, and often a higher pH than some conventional beers, the product may be more vulnerable to microbial growth if not handled correctly.

This is why pasteurisation, sterile filtration, hygienic filling, oxygen control, and cold-chain decisions matter. The exact approach depends on the brewery, product, and packaging format, but the principle is the same: a low-ABV product with residual extract must be treated with care.

Packaging can also expose weaknesses. A beer that tastes balanced in tank may become dull, sweet, oxidised, or unstable after several weeks in can, keg, or bottle. Shelf-life testing is therefore not optional. It is part of the production method.

For breweries entering the category, this is often the hidden challenge. Making a promising pilot batch is one thing. Producing a stable packaged product, at the correct ABV, with consistent flavour over time, is a much more demanding task.

Choosing the right method

The right production method depends on the brewery's scale, equipment, target ABV, brand positioning, and tolerance for process complexity.

Smaller breweries often start with fermentation control because it requires less capital investment. This can work well for certain styles, especially where the product is designed around freshness, hop character, acidity, fruit, or a naturally lighter body. It is usually the fastest route to market.

Larger breweries, or breweries aiming for closer parity with standard beer, are more likely to consider dealcoholisation. Vacuum distillation and membrane systems both allow a fuller fermentation before alcohol removal, which can produce a more complete beer profile. The cost is higher, but the quality ceiling is often higher as well.

There is also a middle ground. Some breweries develop low-strength beers rather than fully alcohol-free products. Others contract the dealcoholisation step externally, at least at the beginning. Some use blending strategies to balance flavour while keeping capital expenditure under control.

The commercial decision should follow the product. A 0.5% lager designed for national retail, a 1.0% draught pale ale for pubs, and a specialist alcohol-free IPA for direct-to-consumer sales may all require different approaches.

What brewers should watch

Low- and non-alcoholic beer is now a serious part of the brewing market, but it is also technically unforgiving. The absence of alcohol removes one of beer's natural balancing and stabilising components. That makes recipe design, process control, and packaging discipline more important, not less.

The first point to watch is ABV control. The lower the target, the tighter the process needs to be. Fermentation drift, blending variation, and refermentation can all create problems.

The second is flavour balance. Reduced alcohol changes the way sweetness, bitterness, hop aroma, carbonation, and body are perceived. A recipe cannot simply be copied from a standard beer and scaled down.

The third is stability. Low alcohol, residual extract, and oxygen pickup are a difficult combination. Pasteurisation, filtration, hygienic filling, and shelf-life testing need to be considered from the start.

The fourth is cost. Low- and non-alcoholic beer is not automatically cheaper to make than standard beer. In many cases, it is more expensive because it requires additional processing, tighter quality control, specialised yeast, more product development, or dedicated equipment. Industry commentary has repeatedly noted that non-alcoholic beer production can be more expensive than conventional brewing, despite the lower alcohol content.

For breweries, the practical lesson is clear. Low- and non-alcoholic beer should be treated as its own production category, not as a weaker version of standard beer. The strongest products are designed from the beginning around the final ABV, flavour profile, stability requirements, and route to market.
