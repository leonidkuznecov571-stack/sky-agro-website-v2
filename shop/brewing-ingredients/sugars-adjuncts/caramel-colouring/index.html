Caramel Colouring is a food grade liquid colourant produced through the controlled heating of carbohydrates, supplied as a concentrated dark liquid for direct addition to wort or finished beer. It provides a precise, repeatable means of adjusting beer colour independently of the grist, allowing brewers to achieve target colour without committing further dark malt to the recipe. As coloring agents are virtually free of aroma and flavour at typical addition rates, it can be used to deliver bold visual character without contributing the roasted, astringent, or chocolate notes that come from chocolate malt, roasted barley, or black malt.

This product has two principal uses in brewing. First, as a final correction tool, where small additions are made to bring a beer onto a target colour specification, particularly useful where natural variation in malt deliveries or process conditions has produced a batch that falls short of the intended colour. Second, as a recipe ingredient in its own right, where it allows brewers to formulate dark-coloured beers that retain a milder, less roasted flavour profile, an approach widely used in some commercial styles such as schwarzbier and certain dark lagers, as well as for converting a base lager into a darker product without re-formulating the grist.

Typical usage is highly variable depending on style and starting colour, but additions of 0.01-0.075% (by volume of finished beer) are common, equivalent to approximately 0.1-7.5ml per litre. Lagers typically require lower additions than ales, while substantial colour shifts (for example, converting a pale lager to a bock-coloured beer) require higher rates. As a guide, trial additions should be made at small scale and colour assessed against a reference before committing to full production volumes. Add at the kettle, post-fermentation, or at packaging depending on process and preference.

Caramel Colouring is suitable for:

  • Final colour correction to meet style or brand specifications
  • Achieving target colour without additional dark malt character
  • Producing dark-coloured beers with milder roasted flavour
  • Maintaining batch-to-batch colour consistency
  • Converting a base beer into a darker variant (e.g. lager to dark lager)
  • Recipes targeting specific commercial colour profiles
  • Compensating for malt variation across deliveries and harvests
